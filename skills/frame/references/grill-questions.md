# Grill Questions — Probing Patterns by Dimension

A catalog of question patterns for the Frame skill's Grill to Readiness interview (Step 6). Organized by assessment dimension to support the minimum coverage rule: at least one question per unresolved section of the Frame Brief.

Each question is designed to probe beyond surface claims, grounded in realistic organizational dynamics, and free of invented dates or fabricated scenarios.

---

## Dimension A: Stakeholder Intent & Hidden Incentives

Maps to Frame Brief sections: *Intent, Executive interpretation, Intended outcomes*

### Q1. Winner-Loser Map
Whose interests are best served by this plan succeeding — and whose interests are best served by it failing or stalling?

*Why this matters*: Every plan creates winners and losers, even if the "losers" are merely people whose existing workflow gets disrupted. If the plan doesn't name the stakeholders who lose something, it hasn't accounted for active or passive resistance. This forces the author to map the political economy of the change, not just the org chart.

### Q2. Sponsor Survivability
If the person who originally commissioned this work left the organization tomorrow, what in the document would survive their departure — and what would collapse because it depended on their unstated beliefs, relationships, or authority?

*Why this matters*: This distinguishes *institutional commitment* from *individual sponsorship*. Plans that are actually sponsor-dependent but present themselves as organizationally grounded are fragile in a way that no amount of execution rigor can fix.

### Q3. Shadow Success Criteria
What outcome would cause the stakeholders to quietly decide this was a success even if the stated metrics were never met?

*Why this matters*: This exposes the real success criteria — the ones people will actually use to judge the work — versus the formal ones written into the plan. The gap between these is the space where post-hoc rationalization lives, and a robust plan either closes that gap or names it explicitly.

## Dimension B: Causal Logic & Reverse Inference

Maps to Frame Brief sections: *Logic / operating model, Key assumptions, Dependencies*

### Q4. Falsifiability Test
If we observed the *exact opposite* of one of your key leading indicators six months in, what would that tell you — and would you be able to distinguish "the plan is wrong" from "the plan is right but the indicator is noisy"?

*Why this matters*: This tests whether the plan's measurement framework is falsifiable. A plan where every possible observation confirms the theory is not a plan — it's a belief system. The question forces the author to pre-commit to what evidence would constitute disconfirmation, which is the difference between adaptive management and confirmation bias.

### Q5. Control Boundary
Walk backward from the desired outcome through each dependency chain. At which link does the logic shift from "within our control" to "requires someone else's decision or permission" — and what is the consequence if that link breaks?

*Why this matters*: Most plans present a seamless chain from activity to outcome. In reality, there is almost always a *control boundary* — a handoff to another team, a procurement decision, a regulatory approval — where the plan's logic becomes contingent on an external actor. This question maps that boundary explicitly and estimates the *qualitative cost of the break* (delay magnitude, rework scope, political capital burn) without inventing a date.

### Q6. Worst-Case Latent Assumption
What is the single assumption in this plan that, if wrong, causes the most damage that cannot be detected until the latest stage?

*Why this matters*: This is the latent-assumption stress test. It distinguishes between assumptions that fail fast and visibly (manageable) and assumptions that fail silently until late-stage integration or rollout (catastrophic). The evidence ledger captures the assumption; this question captures its *failure latency*.

## Dimension C: Evidence Quality & Epistemic Rigor

Maps to Frame Brief sections: *Evidence ledger, Unknowns, Potential contradictions*

### Q7. Verified-to-Assumed Downgrade
For the claim you classified as "Verified" that carries the most weight in the argument, what would it take for you to reclassify it as "Assumed" — and has that condition ever occurred in your organization?

*Why this matters*: This directly tests the evidence hierarchy. "Verified" often means "someone in authority said it confidently" rather than "supported by primary-source evidence." Asking what would downgrade the classification forces the author to examine the actual foundation of their strongest claim, not just label it.

### Q8. Kill Condition
What piece of information, if it existed, would make you abandon this approach entirely — and what is your confidence that it does *not* exist?

*Why this matters*: This is the inversion technique applied within Frame. It asks for the *disconfirming evidence* that would be fatal. A plan that can't name its own kill condition hasn't been honestly stress-tested, even at the reconstruction stage.

## Dimension D: Readiness & Decision Architecture

Maps to Frame Brief sections: *Decisions requiring confirmation, Readiness for Break*

### Q9. Reversibility Topology
Which decisions in this plan are reversible at low cost, and which are effectively irreversible once made? Are the irreversible decisions front-loaded (early in the sequence) or back-loaded?

*Why this matters*: This maps the plan's *decision topology* — not what decisions are being made, but their reversibility and sequencing. A plan that front-loads irreversible decisions (e.g., technology platform choice, organizational restructure) while deferring reversible ones (e.g., communication templates, training schedule) has an asymmetric risk profile. The frame brief should explicitly call out whether the sequencing is appropriate or whether reversible decisions should be moved earlier to create learning opportunities before irreversible ones are locked in.

---

## Usage Notes

* These are question *patterns*, not a script. Adapt the wording to the specific artifact under review.
* Not every question applies to every document. Use the minimum coverage rule to select at least one question per unresolved Frame Brief section; skip dimensions whose corresponding sections have no gaps.
* Questions marked with a dimension mapping should be selected when the corresponding Frame Brief section contains unresolved gaps.
* All questions use qualitative framing — no invented dates, numbers, or fictional scenarios.
